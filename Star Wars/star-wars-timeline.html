Section 1

Executive Guide

Release Order vs. Chronological Order

Star Wars was not made in story order. George Lucas released the saga beginning with what is now Episode IV, and the prequel and sequel trilogies were produced decades apart. Release order follows the films and series as they were first published — the order in which the creative team intended audiences to encounter the story, including intentional reveals and surprises. Chronological order arranges all titles by their in-universe story setting, from the earliest era to the most recent, regardless of when they were made.

Neither order is definitively "correct." Release order preserves the original dramatic structure and the mystery around Darth Vader's identity and the Emperor's plans. Chronological order gives context to prequels and understands political history before it becomes relevant, but risks spoiling major reveals — particularly the Darth Vader revelation — for first-time viewers who watch Episode III before Episode IV.

The BBY/ABY Dating System

In-universe, Star Wars does not have a single, universal calendar consistent across all species and systems. The BBY/ABY convention (Before/After the Battle of Yavin) is a fan-facing shorthand, used in reference materials and Wookieepedia, not a formal in-universe calendar. The Battle of Yavin — the climax of A New Hope — serves as the zero point because it is the best-known fixed event in the saga. Official Lucasfilm materials sometimes use it implicitly; in-universe characters do not say "32 BBY."

All BBY/ABY dates in this document are based on published Lucasfilm reference materials and the fan-standard Wookieepedia dating convention. Dates marked c. are approximate. Dates marked Uncertain lack an official placement and are inferred from context.

Current Canon vs. Legends

Prior to April 2014, the Star Wars Expanded Universe (EU) — comprising novels, comics, games, and other materials — was organized into tiers of canonicity. In April 2014, Lucasfilm declared a clean continuity reset: everything except the six theatrical films, The Clone Wars animated series, and newly created material would be designated Legends — an officially acknowledged alternate continuity that remains in print but no longer informs new stories.

The current canon consists of everything produced under the post-2014 continuity: the sequel trilogy, Disney+ live-action series, the post-2014 animated series, and all story-group-approved novels, comics, and games. This document covers only on-screen canon content. Legends material is addressed separately in Section 7.

Scope of This Document

This reference covers all officially canonical, on-screen Star Wars storytelling: theatrical films, live-action series, animated series, animated anthology shorts, and relevant specials. Star Wars: Visions is included with its non-canon status clearly marked. The 2003 Clone Wars micro-series is included as Legends. Novels, comics, games, audio dramas, and fan works are excluded from the main timeline and referenced only where a screen title directly depends on them. The research date is September 2026; announced projects with dates beyond that may be incomplete.

Section 4

Era Summaries

Each era is an official Lucasfilm storytelling designation used in reference materials and the StarWars.com Databank. BBY/ABY ranges are based on primary on-screen content and may not capture every edge.

High Republic c. 400–100 BBY (on screen: c. 232–100 BBY)
Approx. years
~400 to ~100 BBY (screen content: ~232 to ~100 BBY)
Political state
The Galactic Republic is at its zenith — prosperous, expansionist, and largely peaceful. The frontier of the Outer Rim is being colonised and protected by the Republic and Jedi.
Dominant factions
Galactic Republic; Jedi Order (at full strength, numbering in the thousands); Nihil (pirates/raiders); Hutt Clans
Force traditions
The Jedi Order at its most idealised; diverse Force-using cultures on the frontier; early hints of dark-side sects
Central conflicts
The Nihil threat in the Outer Rim (novels/comics); the secret emergence of a Sith tradition by ~100 BBY (The Acolyte)
Key screen titles
Young Jedi Adventures (c. 232 BBY); The Acolyte (c. 100 BBY)
Transition
The era ends as the Sith secretly establish themselves in the shadows. By 32 BBY (Episode I), their presence has grown to the point of open action. The Rule of Two, established by Darth Bane ~1,000 BBY (Legends; implied in canon), ensures the Sith operate in secret.
Fall of the Jedi 32–19 BBY
Approx. years
32–19 BBY
Political state
The Galactic Republic is increasingly fractured by political corruption, Trade Federation disputes, and the growing Separatist movement. Chancellor Palpatine accumulates extraordinary emergency powers.
Dominant factions
Galactic Republic; Jedi Order; Confederacy of Independent Systems (Separatists); Grand Army of the Republic (clone army); Sith (in secret until Episode I)
Force traditions
The Jedi Order at its structural peak but philosophically rigid; the Sith operating in secret via the Rule of Two; the Nightsisters of Dathomir
Central conflicts
Clone Wars (22–19 BBY): the Republic and Jedi fight the Separatist military; simultaneously, Darth Sidious manipulates both sides to manufacture his own rise to absolute power
Key screen titles
The Phantom Menace (32 BBY); Attack of the Clones (22 BBY); The Clone Wars film and series (22–19 BBY); Tales of the Jedi (spans into this era); Revenge of the Sith (19 BBY)
Transition
Order 66 nearly destroys the Jedi in a single night. Palpatine declares himself Emperor; the Republic becomes the Galactic Empire. Anakin Skywalker becomes Darth Vader. The surviving Jedi go into hiding, and the age of Imperial rule begins.
Reign of the Empire 19–0 BBY
Approx. years
~19–0 BBY (~19 years)
Political state
The Galactic Empire rules the galaxy through military force, fear, and bureaucratic control. Regional governors (Moffs) administer sectors; the Senate is stripped of power and eventually disbanded. The Death Star is secretly under construction for most of the era.
Dominant factions
Galactic Empire (ISB, Imperial military, Inquisitorius); early Rebel cells (coalescing into the Rebel Alliance by ~1 BBY); crime syndicates (Crimson Dawn, Pyke Syndicate, Hutt Clans)
Force traditions
The Inquisitorius: former Jedi turned dark-side agents hunting Jedi survivors; Darth Vader and the Emperor; isolated Jedi survivors in hiding (Obi-Wan, Yoda, Kanan Jarrus); Maul operating independently
Central conflicts
Imperial consolidation; Jedi survival; the gradual formation of organised armed resistance; Imperial atrocities on occupied worlds; the arms race toward the Death Star
Key screen titles
The Bad Batch (19–17 BBY); Maul – Shadow Lord (c. 17–14 BBY); Solo (c. 13–10 BBY); Obi-Wan Kenobi (9 BBY); Andor (5–0 BBY); Rebels (5–0 BBY); Tales of the Empire (multi)
Transition
The Rebel Alliance formally coalesces in the year before A New Hope (~1 BBY). The Death Star's completion triggers the events of Rogue One and immediately thereafter Episode IV — the Battle of Yavin, which destroys the Death Star and marks the beginning of the Age of Rebellion.
Age of Rebellion 0 BBY – c. 5 ABY
Approx. years
~0 BBY to ~4–5 ABY (approximately 4–5 years)
Political state
Open galactic civil war between the Rebel Alliance and the Galactic Empire. The Rebellion operates from hidden bases and mobile fleets; the Empire fields its full military might including the Death Star and later the second Death Star.
Dominant factions
Rebel Alliance; Galactic Empire; Hutt Clans (largely neutral)
Force traditions
Luke Skywalker's self-taught and Yoda-guided emergence as a Jedi; Darth Vader active as the Emperor's enforcer; the Emperor himself wielding the dark side; Obi-Wan Kenobi as a Force ghost
Central conflicts
The Galactic Civil War; the Battle of Yavin (0 BBY); the Battle of Hoth (3 ABY); the Battle of Endor and the fall of the Emperor (4 ABY)
Key screen titles
Rogue One (0 BBY); A New Hope (0 BBY); The Empire Strikes Back (3 ABY); Return of the Jedi (4 ABY)
Transition
The Emperor is killed at Endor; Vader redeemed and dies. The Empire is shattered but not destroyed — Imperial remnants persist for years. The New Republic is established, and a new democratic government attempts to unite the galaxy. This fragile transition defines the New Republic era.
New Republic c. 5–34 ABY
Approx. years
~5–34 ABY (~29 years; screen content concentrated c. 9 ABY)
Political state
The New Republic governs most of the galaxy but is ideologically divided — many senators resist maintaining a strong military, scarred by the memory of Imperial overreach. Imperial Warlords hold scattered territories. The New Republic struggles to project authority in the Outer Rim.
Dominant factions
New Republic; Imperial remnants (various warlords); resurgent Mandalorian clans; the Bounty Hunters' Guild; crime syndicates; the First Order (forming in secret in the Unknown Regions by ~20 ABY)
Force traditions
Luke Skywalker attempting to rebuild the Jedi Order (ultimately ending in disaster c. 28 ABY); Ahsoka Tano operating independently; dark-side practitioners in the Unknown Regions associated with the nascent First Order
Central conflicts
Imperial remnant suppression; Grogu's protection and training; the Mandalorian civil war and reclamation of Mandalore; Thrawn's return and planned conquest; the galaxy's political instability enabling the First Order's growth
Key screen titles
The Mandalorian (c. 9 ABY); The Book of Boba Fett (c. 9 ABY); Ahsoka Season 1 (c. 9 ABY); Skeleton Crew (c. 9 ABY); The Mandalorian and Grogu (c. 9–10 ABY)
Transition
The New Republic is complacent and unwilling to act against the First Order's growing power. The Hosnian Cataclysm (~34 ABY) — the destruction of the New Republic's capital and fleet by Starkiller Base — effectively ends the New Republic and plunges the galaxy back into open war.
Rise of the First Order c. 34–35 ABY
Approx. years
~34–35 ABY (the sequel trilogy spans roughly one year)
Political state
The New Republic is destroyed by the First Order's Starkiller Base weapon. The Resistance — a small private force funded by General Leia Organa — is the only organised opposition. The First Order, formed from Imperial remnants in the Unknown Regions, has emerged as a dominant military power.
Dominant factions
First Order (Supreme Leader Snoke, then Kylo Ren; the Final Order under Palpatine); the Resistance; scattered New Republic survivors
Force traditions
Rey's awakening and training (self-taught, tutored by Leia and Luke's writings/Force ghost); Kylo Ren as the Supreme Leader's enforcer; the Knights of Ren; Palpatine's return via Exegol and the Final Order Sith loyalists
Central conflicts
The destruction of the Hosnian system; the Battle of Crait; the Battle of Exegol; the fall of Palpatine and the Final Order; Kylo Ren's redemption as Ben Solo
Key screen titles
Star Wars Resistance (34–35 ABY); The Force Awakens (34 ABY); The Last Jedi (34 ABY); The Rise of Skywalker (35 ABY)
Transition
Palpatine is defeated at Exegol; Ben Solo dies saving Rey. The Final Order fleet is destroyed. The galaxy celebrates, but no clear successor government is established on screen. What comes next — a New Jedi Order, a rebuilt Republic — is an open question in current canon.

Section 6

Alternate Realities, Force Visions & Temporal Anomalies

How Star Wars Generally Treats the Force, Destiny, and Visions

Star Wars does not operate on a formal multiverse model. The Force is presented as a mystical, living energy field that connects all life — it can show visions of possible futures, echoes of the past, and metaphysical experiences that transcend physical reality. Not every Force vision creates a separate timeline. Official Lucasfilm sources treat most visions and anomalous Force experiences as aspects of a single continuous timeline, not branches.

Official position: Lucasfilm Story Group has not described Star Wars as a multiverse. The World Between Worlds is a confirmed, in-universe location that enables true temporal intervention in specific instances; it is not a general portal to alternate histories. All confirmed timeline changes via the World Between Worlds remain in the primary canon continuity.

The World Between Worlds

First introduced in Star Wars Rebels Season 4 ("A World Between Worlds"), the World Between Worlds is a mystical plane of existence accessible through a portal on Lothal. It exists outside of conventional time and space, containing doorways into moments throughout history.

  • Confirmed canonical intervention: Ezra Bridger pulls Ahsoka Tano from the moment of her duel with Darth Vader on Malachor (Rebels Season 2 finale, "Twilight of the Apprentice"), effectively removing her from that point in time and depositing her into the Season 4 timeframe. This is a confirmed, canon change to the timeline — Ahsoka's survival is explained by this intervention.
  • Ezra's encounter with the Emperor: Emperor Palpatine attempts to use the World Between Worlds to alter history (specifically to prevent the Battle of Yavin / save the Empire). Ezra and Ahsoka collectively prevent this. The Emperor's attempt fails — no timeline alteration occurs from his effort. Interpretive: the exact mechanism Palpatine intended is not fully explained on screen.
  • Nature of the space: Within it, figures from the past and future are visible through doorways but not directly interactable. The space is connected to the Cosmic Force. Its precise rules are not fully defined on screen.
  • Ahsoka (series): The World Between Worlds is referenced but does not serve as a plot mechanism in Season 1. Uncertain — Season 2 scope is not yet known.

Mortis (The Clone Wars, Seasons 3–4)

The "Mortis arc" (The Clone Wars Seasons 3–4, episodes "Overlords," "Altar of Mortis," "Ghosts of Mortis") takes Anakin, Obi-Wan, and Ahsoka to a mysterious realm where three powerful Force-wielders — the Father, the Son (dark side), and the Daughter (light side) — maintain the balance of the Force.

  • The Daughter dies to save Ahsoka's life, transferring her life energy into Ahsoka. This is considered a canonical event contributing to Ahsoka's unusual resilience and Force connection.
  • The Father shows Anakin a vision of his future as Darth Vader — but then wipes his memory of the vision. This is presented in-universe as something that actually happens, not a dream.
  • The arc is widely interpreted as allegorical and mythological in tone. It is not a separate timeline or alternate reality — these events occur within the primary canon continuity.
  • Interpretive: Whether Mortis is a literal physical location or a Force-created state is left deliberately ambiguous by the showrunners.

The Mortis Gods and Ahsoka Season 1

The imagery and themes of Mortis — particularly the Son and Daughter — reappear symbolically in Ahsoka Season 1 through the Nightsisters and certain visual motifs. Whether this constitutes a direct narrative connection is Interpretive — not confirmed by official sources.

Major Force Visions and Prophetic Experiences

These are presented as occurring within the primary timeline, not as alternate realities:

  • Luke's cave vision on Dagobah (Empire Strikes Back): Luke faces a vision of himself defeating Vader, only to see Vader's face in the helmet. Widely interpreted as a warning or psychological truth about Luke's own darkness. Not a separate timeline.
  • Rey and Kylo Ren's Force bond (The Last Jedi / The Rise of Skywalker): A dyad in the Force creates a genuine cross-space connection. Not a vision — confirmed real by both characters and by the in-universe Force mechanics established in the films.
  • Anakin's prophetic visions of Padmé's death (Revenge of the Sith): Arguably self-fulfilling. Not a separate timeline; they occur within the primary continuity and drive the plot.
  • Ahsoka's World Between Worlds experience with Anakin (Ahsoka Season 1): Ahsoka experiences a Force-mediated encounter with Anakin that revisits their history. Interpretive — whether this is a literal Force encounter or a psychological experience is left ambiguous on screen.

Star Wars: Visions — Official Non-Canon Anthology

Star Wars: Visions (Volume 1: 2021, Volume 2: 2023) is an officially licensed anthology of animated short films in which Lucasfilm invited animation studios to create their own Star Wars-inspired stories. These are explicitly non-canon — they do not occur within the primary continuity and are not intended to. They are celebratory creative expressions of the Star Wars aesthetic by artists worldwide, not story-group-approved additions to the timeline.

Not to be confused with canon anthology series (Tales of the Jedi, Tales of the Empire, Tales of the Underworld), which are fully canonical and produced directly by Lucasfilm Animation.

Section 7

Legends Companion Guide

This section covers a separate continuity. Legends material does not inform current canon storytelling and should never be merged with the timeline in Sections 1–6. It is presented here as a reference for viewers who encountered these stories and want to understand their relationship to current canon.

What Is Legends?

From 1976 through April 2014, an expansive collection of novels, comics, games, and other materials built out the Star Wars universe under various "levels of canonicity" (C-canon, G-canon, etc.). In April 2014, Lucasfilm declared a clean continuity reset. Everything except the six theatrical films (Episodes I–VI), The Clone Wars animated series, and any newly produced material would be relabeled Legends — an officially acknowledged alternate continuity. Legends material remains in print and sale, but no longer informs or constrains current story development.

What the 2014 Reset Did — and Did Not — Mean

  • Did mean: Characters, events, and history established only in Legends works (Mara Jade, Jacen Solo, the Old Republic era as depicted in KOTOR, the New Jedi Order novels) are no longer canonical facts. New stories may re-use names, concepts, or ideas from Legends without being bound by them.
  • Did not mean: The six films or The Clone Wars were changed. Material that appeared in those works remained canon regardless of the reset.
  • Did not mean: Legends is "wrong" or worthless — it is simply a different continuity. Many fans hold both in equal regard.
  • Did not mean: Canon and Legends will never overlap — new canon occasionally re-canonises a Legends character or concept (e.g., Grand Admiral Thrawn was Legends until his introduction into Rebels in 2016 made him canon).

Original Version vs. Special Editions

The original trilogy has been revised across every major home video format — each release introduced new changes on top of the previous version, compounding across decades. The Disney+ version contains at least one change not present on any physical media release. The original theatrical cuts have never received an official HD release; the closest is the non-anamorphic 2006 DVD bonus disc. This document does not adjudicate between versions — it describes what exists and where.

What Changed and When

1997 Special Editions — all three films
  • A New Hope: Mos Eisley approach replaced with CGI overhaul; larger CGI dewbacks added; deleted CGI Jabba scene restored (Jabba now confronts Han before departure); Greedo revised to shoot first (Han still fires simultaneously); Biggs restored at Anchorhead (brief)
  • Empire Strikes Back: Cloud City exterior windows added (shows cityscape); Wampa scenes extended; Emperor hologram changed to Ian McDiarmid's face (voice still Clive Revill)
  • Return of the Jedi: Lapti Nek replaced by "Jedi Rocks" with new CGI musical number at Jabba's palace; Sarlacc given a CGI beak; victory celebration scenes extended across multiple planets (Coruscant, Naboo, Tatooine, Bespin, Endor); Anakin Force ghost remains Sebastian Shaw
2004 DVD — additional changes over 1997 SE
  • A New Hope: Greedo/Han timing adjusted (both fire near-simultaneously, but Greedo still visually first); further CGI refinements throughout Mos Eisley; Jabba's tail no longer awkwardly passes through Han
  • Empire Strikes Back: Emperor hologram scene fully reshot — Ian McDiarmid performs new dialogue aligning with prequel continuity; Clive Revill voice replaced entirely
  • Return of the Jedi: Sebastian Shaw's Anakin Force ghost replaced with Hayden Christensen (the most controversial change); Yoda's eyes digitally retouched
  • All films: Heavy DNR processing (smoothed grain, controversial); color timing adjustments
2011 Blu-ray — additional changes over 2004 DVD
  • Return of the Jedi: Vader now shouts "No! No!" before throwing the Emperor into the shaft — inserted from prequel-established Vader voice performance; Ewok eyes digitally widened/made more expressive
  • All films: Full HD presentation (1080p); improved color grading vs. the 2004 DNR master; various minor CGI touch-ups and cleanup; Sarlacc beak, Hayden ghost, all prior SE and 2004 changes carried forward
Disney+ (2019) — exclusive change, no physical equivalent
  • A New Hope: "Maclunkey!" — George Lucas added a new line of dialogue for Greedo spoken immediately before the cantina confrontation. Greedo says "Maclunkey!" then draws and fires; Han fires back. This change was added at Disney+ launch in 2019 and exists only in the streaming version. It is not present on any Blu-ray, DVD, or earlier format.
  • All films: 4K HDR presentation (upscaled from 2011 master); based on 2011 Blu-ray with all prior changes carried forward plus the above
As of 2026, no 4K UHD physical media release of the original trilogy exists. Disney+ is the only source for HDR presentation of these films.
If you want to watch the closest thing to the original cuts: The 2006 DVD bonus discs are the only official option — sourced from the 1993 LaserDisc master, standard definition, non-anamorphic letterbox. Fan restoration projects (most notably Harmy's "Despecialized Edition") exist and are widely discussed online but are unofficial. The original theatrical film prints are held by the Academy Film Archive and have been screened publicly on rare occasions, but no high-quality digital transfer of the original versions has been officially released.

Evolving Production Choices and Retroactive Continuity

Several creative decisions in later productions create tension with earlier material. This document describes these as evolving production choices unless official sources explicitly describe them as corrections or errors.

  • Obi-Wan Kenobi and Ahsoka's non-recognition: A New Hope implies Obi-Wan had not seen a Jedi in a long time. Obi-Wan Kenobi (2022) shows them interacting as recently as ~9 BBY, and Ahsoka is alive and a known figure in the Rebel network. The films' dialogue is interpreted by production teams as referring to active Jedi, not former Padawans. Interpretive — not explicitly addressed on screen.
  • Leia's memory of her mother: In Return of the Jedi, Leia says she has "just images, feelings" of her mother, who was "very beautiful, kind, but sad." In Revenge of the Sith, Padmé dies immediately after childbirth. Lucasfilm has not officially resolved this; fan explanations include the Force, implanted memories, or the films simply being made decades apart. Unresolved.
  • Darth Maul's survival: Maul was cut in half in Episode I. His survival, first shown in The Clone Wars Season 4, was a retcon introduced by Dave Filoni and approved by George Lucas. It is confirmed canon; the in-universe explanation involves Force-fueled survival and mechanical legs.
  • Cassian Andor's age: Andor (series) established Cassian was taken by the Separatists as a young child during the Clone Wars (~22–19 BBY). This makes him approximately 26–28 in Rogue One — consistent with Diego Luna's appearance but not explicitly stated in the film.
  • The Ahsoka/Vader duel resolution: Rebels Season 2 showed the duel's initiation; Season 4's World Between Worlds revealed Ahsoka survived. This is not a retcon — the original broadcast of the S2 finale left her fate deliberately ambiguous. The Season 4 explanation is the intended canonical resolution.
