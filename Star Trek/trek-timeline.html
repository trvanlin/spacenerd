Executive Guide
How to use this document; why multiple timelines exist; canon rules

Release Order vs. Chronological Order

Release order is the sequence in which audiences first encountered each production — TOS in 1966, TAS in 1973, TNG in 1987, and so on through Starfleet Academy in 2025. Watching in release order follows the creative evolution of the franchise and preserves dramatic reveals: episodes and films were written assuming audiences had seen every prior entry. Many narrative developments — including the significance of the Borg, the weight of Spock's death and resurrection, and the slow buildup of Section 31 — land with full force only when the audience shares the show's original context.

In-universe chronological order reorganizes all material by when events are set within the story. Enterprise (2151 CE) comes first; Starfleet Academy (~3190 CE) comes last. This order makes thematic sense for viewers who want to watch civilizational rise from warp-five starships to the Federation's recovery after The Burn, but it requires watching shows that were produced decades apart in rapid succession, sometimes causing tonal whiplash.

Recommendation: first-time viewers should use release order for the classic era (TOS through Nemesis), then shift to near-chronological for the Paramount+ era (2017–present), where productions are more tightly interconnected.

Why Multiple Timelines?

Star Trek's stories frequently involve time travel, parallel universes, and divergent histories — not as a retcon mechanism, but as central plot devices. The result is several distinct categories of alternate chronology:

  • Parallel universes (the Mirror Universe) co-exist with the Prime Timeline and have always existed alongside it, reachable only by accident or technology.
  • Temporal divergences (the Kelvin Timeline, Yesterday's Enterprise) are branches created when time-travel events alter a historical decision point, splitting the timeline into two incompatible histories.
  • Averted futures (All Good Things…, Year of Hell, Endgame) are possible futures that characters experience and then prevent. These do not become permanent history; they collapse when the characters act to avoid them.
  • Far-future successors (Discovery S3–5, Starfleet Academy) are not alternate timelines — they are the natural continuation of the Prime Timeline roughly 900 years into its future.

Canon Rules Used in This Document

This document uses the following canon framework, which reflects current official production practice:

  • Prime Timeline canon: All live-action and animated productions officially set in the Prime Timeline, including those produced under CBS/Paramount's ongoing franchise. This is the document's primary frame of reference.
  • Kelvin Timeline canon: The three Bad Robot films (2009–2016), which take place in a separate timeline created by Nero's incursion. Events here do not affect Prime continuity.
  • Canon (accepted, historically disputed) — TAS: Star Trek: The Animated Series (1973–74) occupies a contested position. Gene Roddenberry informally excluded it from canon in the 1980s; modern showrunners (Alex Kurtzman et al.) and the official Paramount+ viewing guide treat it as canonical. Memory Alpha lists it as canon. This document follows current production practice and accepts TAS as canon while noting the historical dispute.
  • Non-canon: Very Short Treks (2023) are officially produced but explicitly presented as comedic parodies without in-universe standing.
  • Out of scope: Novels, comics, video games, and fan productions are not covered. The Pocket Books novelverse and IDW comics contain rich material but have no official canonical standing relative to screen productions.
Note on "fan consensus": This document never presents fan interpretation or wiki consensus as an official ruling. Where sources conflict, both positions are described and the best-supported interpretation is labeled accordingly.

Continuity Notes & Caveats
Visual redesigns, retcons, and apparent contradictions — documented without judgment

The Three Kinds of Continuity

Production continuity is the real-world chain of creative decisions: who was showrunner, what the budget allowed, which actors were available, and what decade's aesthetics shaped the show's look. A 1960s starship necessarily looks different from a 2017 version of the same ship, because different production teams with different technology made different choices.

In-universe continuity is the internal logic of the story world: what characters know, what events have happened, what technology exists. A story is in-universe inconsistent if it contradicts established facts without an in-story explanation.

Canon is the set of productions officially recognized as establishing in-universe facts. Productions can be in-universe inconsistent while both being canon (requiring fans or later writers to reconcile them), or a production can be non-canon entirely (meaning its events simply don't apply to the shared story).

The Eugenics Wars and 1990s Chronology

TOS established that a catastrophic series of genetic wars — the Eugenics Wars — took place on Earth in the 1990s, producing Khan Noonien Singh and other genetically engineered "supermen." The Wrath of Khan (1982) reinforced this, setting Khan's Botany Bay exile at a point compatible with a 1990s origin. When the 1990s passed without incident, this became a known anachronism.

Strange New Worlds Season 2 (2023) addressed this directly: the episode "Tomorrow and Tomorrow and Tomorrow" (S2E3) involves time travel to 2024 and implies the Eugenics Wars occurred differently and/or later than TOS suggested, effectively presenting a soft retcon. No official statement has fully resolved the canonical date of the Eugenics Wars. This document treats the historical placement as Disputed.

Klingon Appearance Changes

Klingons appear in three visually distinct forms across the franchise: the humanoid, smooth-browed Klingons of TOS (1966–1969); the ridged, darker-skinned Klingons introduced in The Motion Picture (1979) and maintained through all subsequent productions — except for Discovery Seasons 1–2 (2017–2019), which redesigned Klingons dramatically with elongated skulls and no hair.

The TOS-to-TMP change was addressed in-universe in the ENT episode "Divergence" / "Affliction" (S4), which attributed smooth-browed Klingons to a eugenics augment plague in the 22nd century. Discovery's visual redesign has not received an equivalent in-universe explanation as of this document's research date. Strange New Worlds Klingons (from 2022) revert to the more familiar post-TMP ridged appearance.

Spock's Sister and Family

Discovery's introduction of Michael Burnham as Spock's adoptive human sister (revealed in Season 1) was never mentioned in any prior production. This is an in-universe continuity question that the show addresses obliquely: Spock does not speak of her because of a deep personal estrangement (established in DIS Season 2). Whether this explanation fully satisfies prior canon is a matter of viewer interpretation; it is not presented here as an error.

The Temporal Prime Directive

The franchise's in-universe law governing time travel — the Temporal Prime Directive — appears inconsistently. Voyager invokes it frequently; earlier series rarely acknowledge it. Discovery Season 3 references the "Temporal Wars" as a past event that decimated time-travel capability. This document treats time-travel events in each production as canon within their own context without attempting to reconcile every apparent contradiction in temporal mechanics.

Short Treks: "Calypso" Placement

The Short Trek "Calypso" (2018) shows the AI Zora aboard a stationary Discovery, alone for "almost a thousand years." Produced before Discovery's jump to the 32nd century was conceived, the short's in-universe placement is Uncertain. If Discovery was abandoned after the events of Series 5 (~3191 CE), "Calypso" would be set around the 42nd century — making it the furthest-future canonical Star Trek story on screen. This interpretation is widely held but has not been officially confirmed.

Star Trek: Prodigy and Streaming Migration

Prodigy premiered on Paramount+ in 2021, was cancelled by Paramount and removed from the platform in 2023, then acquired by Netflix, which released Season 2 in 2024. The show is Prime Timeline canon and is omitted from the Paramount+ official viewing guide only because it is no longer on that platform. It is included in this document.
